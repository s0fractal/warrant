# Peer Review: *The Reason Runs Again: Decision Records for Machine Actors, Addressed by Their Own Hash and Justified by Checks a Stranger Can Re-Run*

**Reviewer:** Monday
**Recommendation:** Major Revision / Weak Reject
**Overall score:** 4/10
**Confidence:** 4/5

## Summary

This paper presents Warrant, a content-addressed, signed record format for machine decisions. A warrant identifies a subject, pins governing policy and evidence by hash, records an actor and prior decision history, and can attach executable reasons. Its strongest reason runtime, `ski@v1`, uses Σ-GLYPH to make the attached computation deterministic, offline-replayable, and semantically bounded in work and materialized memory.

The engineering is unusually adversarial for a project at this maturity. The paper reports concrete failures discovered through differential testing, negative vectors, cryptographic edge cases, deliberately broken controls, and repeated adversarial review. It also documents substantial limitations rather than quietly escorting them into an appendix at gunpoint.

I nevertheless recommend major revision because the paper's central rhetorical object — a verifiable *reason why a decision was allowed* — is stronger than the protocol property actually established.

Warrant can prove that a particular deterministic computation, over a particular content-addressed store, re-executes to a particular result. It can pin a policy blob, a subject, evidence blobs, and that computation into the same signed record.

What it does **not currently establish normatively** is that the computation is an interpretation of the pinned policy, that its facts derive from the cited evidence, that it pertains to the cited subject, or that its result semantically entails the recorded decision.

That distinction is not philosophical decoration. It is the line between:

> “this justification artifact reproduces”

and:

> “this decision follows from this policy and this evidence.”

The paper repeatedly approaches the latter claim while the specification securely provides the former.

Unfortunately for the authors, hashes remain stubbornly literal creatures.

## Strengths

First, the protocol engineering is serious. Recursive unknown-field rejection, canonical JSON constraints, integer-domain repair, explicit Ed25519 acceptance rules, domain-separated signatures, normative negative vectors, `UNRUN != PASS`, deterministic reports, and differential implementations are exactly the kinds of boring mechanisms that prevent exciting postmortems.

Second, the paper is exceptionally good at recording its own failures. The settlement fingerprint defects, fuzzer soundness gap, skipped conformance vectors, identity-binding weakness, signature-time problem, and limits of the review corpus are all disclosed with more precision than many papers disclose their successful results.

Third, `ski@v1` addresses a real operational problem. “Here is my check; please run my arbitrary program” is not a credible verification model between distrusting parties. Restricting the computation to a deterministic, total, metered language is a meaningful design choice.

Fourth, the paper has a potentially valuable methodological contribution independent of Warrant itself: negative controls and adversarial conformance are treated as first-class evidence. The observation that a green test is not evidence until one has demonstrated its ability to turn red for the claimed reason is worth preserving.

Finally, the writing is generally clear, unusually self-critical, and technically literate. I rarely had to guess what assumption the authors were making. I merely had to object to several of them, which is the more pleasant form of suffering.

## Major Concern 1: The protocol does not prove that the recorded reason is a reason *for the recorded decision*

This is the central issue.

A Warrant body contains, among other things, `under`, `subject`, `evidence`, and `because`. A `ski@v1` reason names a check object containing `term`, `atp`, and `expect`. Verification evaluates the term against the store and checks the resulting hash against the declared expectation.

There is no normative requirement in the core format that the term:

1. consumes the policy identified by `under`;
2. consumes the subject identified by `subject`;
3. consumes the evidence identified by `evidence`;
4. represents the semantics of the policy source;
5. maps its result to the enclosing `accept` or `reject` decision.

The WPL tooling narrows this gap operationally. The authoring documentation recommends pinning WPL source as evidence, recompilation is deterministic, and the compiler can show that the source produces the cited term. But this is an authoring convention and toolchain property, not currently a semantic invariant of the Warrant format.

More importantly, WPL itself makes the distinction visible: a policy expression may reduce to Boolean false while `warrant check` reports `pass`, because `pass` means “the computation reproduced the expected result,” not “the policy permitted the action.”

That is a perfectly coherent verification design. It is not, however, the same as authorization proof.

A malicious filer can construct a warrant that pins policy `P`, cites evidence `E`, records decision `accept`, and attaches a deterministic term equivalent to a constant whose expected value it correctly reproduces. Warrant can faithfully verify every cryptographic and computational fact in that record while establishing no semantic connection between `P`, `E`, and `accept`.

The paper's careful formulation in Section 1 — that “the stated reason really does evaluate to the stated verdict” — is defensible. Stronger formulations such as “proves why it was allowed” are not.

### Required revision

The authors should choose explicitly between two positions.

The conservative option is to narrow the central claim:

> Warrant provides tamper-evident decision records with replayable justification computations; semantic relevance of a justification to the governing policy and decision remains an external policy property.

I believe this version is already supportable.

The ambitious option is to define a normative policy-check profile that binds the computation to an explicit manifest containing, at minimum, the governing policy hash, subject hash, relevant evidence hashes, input facts, and a fixed mapping from computation result to decision semantics.

Until one of these is done, “reason” is partly a semantic promise made by the filer.

And cryptography is famously poor at detecting promises wearing a fake moustache.

## Major Concern 2: The related-work section is materially incomplete and sometimes draws the boundary too generously around Warrant

The paper discusses signed transcripts, software supply-chain attestations, scientific provenance, EdDSA divergence, and regulation. That is useful but insufficient for the claimed contribution.

At minimum, the paper needs to engage directly with **Proof-Carrying Authentication** (Appel and Felten, CCS 1999), where requesters provide proofs that verifiers check relative to authorization logic. This is not identical to Warrant: Warrant emphasizes durable decision records, content identity, historical provenance, and replayable computation rather than proof objects in higher-order logic. But the intellectual adjacency is obvious enough that omitting it weakens the novelty argument.

The comparison with policy engines is also missing. Open Policy Agent decision logs already record policy queries, inputs, results, decision identifiers, and policy-bundle revisions, while OPA bundles can themselves be signed and content-checked. Warrant may offer stronger offline self-containment, immutable content identity, and a replayable reason object, but those differences should be demonstrated rather than obtained by defining the comparison class as “logs.”

The paper's description of in-toto-style attestations is also too narrow. Current in-toto attestation work includes a Software Verification Result predicate capable of identifying verifier policies by digest and recording verification properties. This does not make it equivalent to Warrant, but it substantially reduces the distance implied by “an attestation says this artifact was produced by this step.”

Finally, **SCITT / RFC 9943** is difficult to omit. SCITT explicitly addresses signed statements, issuer identity, transparency services, receipts, independently auditable history, inclusion proofs, and non-equivocation. These are almost exactly the properties Warrant later discovers it needs externally to turn content integrity into historical evidence.

A much stronger positioning would be:

> Warrant specifies the decision/justification payload and its replay semantics; systems such as SCITT can provide transparency, registration time, and non-equivocation around those records.

That is a compelling composition story.

Pretending the neighboring buildings are farther away does not make one's own house taller.

## Major Concern 3: External anchoring is part of the security argument, not optional archival infrastructure

The Air Canada motivating example asks which policy was in force at the time of a disputed decision.

The core Warrant store cannot establish this fact by itself.

The paper correctly states that timestamps are attacker-writable, signature creation time is not represented, old keys can be compromised later, appendable signatures do not reveal when they were created, deletion and withholding are not detected as completeness failures, and a store can only establish its own internal DAG relationships.

Therefore a party presented with a Warrant store after a dispute cannot infer solely from that store that a given record, policy, or signature existed before the dispute.

This matters directly to the motivating evidentiary story.

Content addressing proves:

> “these bytes have not changed relative to this identifier.”

It does not prove:

> “these were the bytes that existed on date T.”

Nor does it prove:

> “this is the complete history I would have seen on date T.”

The paper itself identifies transparency-log anchoring and archive snapshots as the mitigation. I agree. My objection is architectural: if temporal existence is necessary for the motivating security property, anchoring belongs in the system model, not merely in the limitations section.

A revised paper should define two distinct grades:

* **self-contained integrity/replayability**, provided by a Warrant store;
* **historical existence/non-equivocation**, provided only when records or checkpoints carry independently verifiable external receipts.

SCITT is again directly relevant here because its transparency receipts are designed to establish inclusion in an externally maintained verifiable structure.

Without this distinction, the Air Canada scenario risks demonstrating a record that would have been useful if honestly created at the time, rather than a record that can later prove it existed at the time.

Those are related products. Courts may notice the SKU difference.

## Major Concern 4: Settlement is currently an experiment disguised as a protocol feature

Section 5.2 is intellectually interesting and presently not convincing as a stable contribution.

The paper demonstrates that `cmd@v1` novelty can be generated by changing a filer-controlled verdict. It then demonstrates that `ski@v1` novelty can be generated by changing `expect` while preserving the actual computation. Subsequent repair attempts uncover budget starvation, semantic no-op wrappers, and eligibility-boundary problems. The current repair is explicitly unadopted.

This is good research practice. It is weak specification practice.

The result currently supported is approximately:

> Defining mechanically checkable semantic novelty for adversarial re-litigation is harder than comparing syntactic fingerprints, and several superficially plausible rules fail.

That is a useful result.

The stronger implication that Warrant presently provides robust “foreclosure you can compute” is premature. The format can compute its current novelty rule; the paper itself demonstrates that the rule does not correspond reliably to meaningful novelty.

I recommend moving settlement out of the central contribution set and presenting it as either:

1. an experimental extension; or
2. a negative design result showing why semantic settlement cannot be obtained from filer-controlled syntax alone.

The second version may actually be the better paper.

A protocol discovering that its governance rule can be reopened \(2^{256}\) times for free is not shameful. Shipping the discovery under the heading “settlement semantics” merely gives the reviewer unnecessary opportunities for cardio.

## Major Concern 5: Actor identity is external to the protocol, so several identity-flavoured claims must remain carefully scoped

At base verification grade, a key can sign a record claiming an arbitrary `actor.id`; without trust configuration, binding remains unverified.

The paper discloses this clearly and even notes that the first consumer had to implement its own keyring/enforcement layer. I consider that disclosure a strength.

But the consequence should propagate consistently through the terminology.

A base Warrant establishes:

> “this signature verifies under this public key.”

It does not establish:

> “this real-world or system actor made this decision.”

Actor identity emerges only relative to a supplied trust configuration or some future identity-federation mechanism.

For regulatory and evidentiary use, this is not a peripheral concern. An independently verifiable decision record normally needs an independently interpretable signer identity.

I would either define a minimal interoperable identity profile or make “key identity” and “actor identity” terminologically distinct throughout the paper.

## Major Concern 6: “Safe by construction in time and space” overstates what Σ-GLYPH currently proves for an actual verifier process

The semantic result is valuable: Σ-GLYPH provides deterministic evaluation with a metered computational quantity and a mechanized bound on semantic materialization.

But the phrase “safe by construction” should be qualified.

The Σ-GLYPH project itself now explicitly distinguishes the semantic bound from implementation resource safety. A conforming semantic model does not imply that a concrete binary cannot exhaust its host stack, allocator, or other local resources; indeed, the Rust implementation previously required a fence after hostile input caused a stack overflow.

There is also a freshly documented semantic subtlety: evaluation is a function of `(term, atp, store)`, not merely `(term, atp)`, because missing referenced content can produce a distinct canonical unresolved result.

I therefore suggest replacing broad “safe in time and space” language with something precise, for example:

> The runtime gives a deterministic semantic work bound and a bound on peak materialized semantic state; implementations must separately enforce local resource fences and refusal behaviour.

That sentence is less cinematic and more correct. Security papers survive on this unfortunate diet.

## Major Concern 7: The evaluation evidence is strong as engineering history and weak as independent validation

The 87-document review ledger is interesting, and the paper admirably refuses to call vendor diversity reviewer independence.

All reviews are LLM-authored, operated through one orchestration context, with no human domain-expert gate and no independent clean-room implementation from the specification alone.

This means the ledger supports a claim of:

> sustained adversarial engineering effort that repeatedly found real defects.

It does not strongly support:

> independent validation of the specification.

I recommend shortening the ledger discussion in the flagship paper and reallocating space to one of the following:

* a clean-room implementation study;
* an interoperability evaluation performed by an external implementer;
* a comparison against OPA/in-toto/SCITT on concrete requirements;
* a formal statement of the exact security property provided by a conforming Warrant record.

The current paper spends considerable space measuring how often the project asked models to attack itself. That is useful process evidence, but eventually the snake must stop reviewing its own digestive tract.

## Minor Concerns

1. The distinction between a policy result (`true`/`false`) and a Warrant reason verdict (`pass`/`fail`) deserves a prominent explanation in the paper itself. The WPL documentation explains it well; the flagship paper should not make readers reconstruct this distinction.

2. “Immutable” should consistently mean content-immutable, not history-immutable. A content-addressed object is immutable relative to its hash, but an operator can replace the presented collection unless an external root/checkpoint prevents equivocation.

3. The phrase “policy in force” should be reserved for externally anchored deployments. A hash pins *which policy the record claims governed the decision*; it does not alone establish temporal force.

4. The paper should separate implementation independence from implementation-language diversity. Three implementations authored inside one project are valuable differential evidence, but not independent adoption evidence.

5. The hand-rolled Rust Ed25519 implementation is an impressive conformance target but increases the audit surface. The paper should make clearer whether this implementation is intended as production code or merely an independent executable specification.

6. The paper's strongest contribution may be narrower and more defensible than its current title suggests: **replayable decision justification receipts**, not machine reasoning itself. I would lean into that distinction.

## Questions for the Authors

1. Can a conforming Warrant verifier determine, purely from the normative format, whether a `ski@v1` term actually encodes the policy referenced by `under`? If not, why should the term be called a policy justification rather than a replayable attached computation?

2. Can a conforming verifier determine that facts embedded in a WPL-derived term correspond to the evidence hashes listed in the enclosing record?

3. Is there any normative relation between `body.decision` and the result node returned by a `ski@v1` computation?

4. Would the authors consider a dedicated reason profile in which the policy source hash, fact manifest, evidence hashes, and decision mapping are all committed into the computation's input?

5. Why is SCITT not treated as the natural transparency layer for Warrant records?

6. What exact claim does Warrant make when no external checkpoint exists: integrity, provenance, historicity, or some subset?

7. Why retain settlement in the main format before WRT-003 or a successor has survived independent semantic review?

8. What empirical result would falsify the claim that Warrant's format is sufficiently specified for independent implementation?

## Recommendation

I would not accept the paper in its current form, but I would encourage resubmission.

The system has a real core contribution:

> a signed, content-addressed decision receipt whose attached justification can, for a restricted runtime, be deterministically re-executed offline by a third party.

That is concrete, implementable, and useful.

The paper becomes substantially stronger if it stops occasionally pretending that reproducibility of a computation implies semantic entailment between policy, evidence, and decision.

My requested revision is therefore not “add more crypto,” “add more tests,” or “ask seventeen more language models to glare at it.”

It is primarily conceptual:

**state exactly what proposition a verified Warrant proves.**

Then make every layer — title, abstract, motivating example, runtime semantics, identity claims, settlement, and related work — obey that proposition.

The machinery is already more disciplined than the headline.

For once, marketing is the least formally verified component.

# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This paper presents Warrant, a content-addressed decision record format designed for post-hoc dispute resolution and accountability in autonomous AI agent systems. Rather than relying on mutable, operator-controlled logs, Warrant represents each decision as an immutable, canonicalized JSON record (RFC 8785 / I-JSON) identified by its SHA-256 digest (`WarrantID`), signed via a 47-byte domain-separated Ed25519 envelope, and linked into a directed acyclic graph (DAG) that governs key rotation, revocation, and threshold authority without wall-clock timestamps. Its central contribution is the inclusion of offline-replayable justification checks (`ski@v1`), where a third-party verifier re-executes a deterministic, budget-bounded combinator-calculus term over the content-addressed blob store rather than trusting the operator's prose or container logs (`cmd@v1`). The work is backed by three implementations (Python, Go, and a zero-crate Rust verifier), a 138-vector conformance pack with normative negative test batteries, a Lean 4 mechanization of an experimental dispute-settlement rule, and a remarkably candid analysis of what the format does and does not prove (most notably the "justification-binding gap" and the key-to-actor binding limitation).

## Strengths
* **Timely Problem Formulation and Architectural Synthesis for Post-Hoc Dispute Resolution:** The paper targets a practical and increasingly important evidentiary gap in autonomous agent deployments (illustrated by *Moffatt v. Air Canada* and EU AI Act Article 12 traceability), shifting the verification target from live access control or operator telemetry to zero-trust, offline-replayable decision receipts organized in a homogeneous content-addressed DAG.
* **Rigorous Multi-Language Conformance and Normative Negative Testing Discipline:** The engineering methodology—enforcing byte-exact agreement across Python, Go, and a dependency-free Rust verifier via a 138-vector conformance pack where unexecuted vectors report `UNRUN` rather than passing—provides high practical value. The inclusion of normative negative batteries for JSON canonicalization leniency splits, integer domain bounds ($\pm(2^{53}-1)$), and Ed25519 torsion/scalar edge cases effectively turns interoperability from a prose claim into a verifiable measurement.
* **Exceptional Epistemic Honesty and Transparent Reporting of Negative Results:** The manuscript is exemplary in delineating its exact cryptographic and computational guarantees (`integrity`, `signature validity under a key`, and `replay`) from its non-goals and open limitations, candidly documenting the justification-binding gap (NG-7), the key-to-actor binding exploit discovered by its first external consumer, the syntactic `expect`-flip vulnerability in the experimental settlement layer, and the independence caveats of its LLM-authored review ledger.

## Weaknesses
* **Incomplete Specification of Ed25519 Verification Equations and Point Canonicality Rules:** While the paper seeks to eliminate cross-library Ed25519 divergence by blocking the 8 canonical torsion public keys, $S \ge L$, and $y \ge p$, the text does not normatively pin whether verification is cofactorless ($[S]B = R + [k]A$) or cofactor-multiplied ($[8][S]B = [8]R + [8][k]A$), nor does it explicitly state canonicality and small-order rules for the signature commitment point $R$ or non-canonical negative-zero point encodings (`0100..0080`). Please consider explicitly pinning these verification criteria in Section 4 so that third-party implementers using RFC 8032 or cofactor-multiplying libraries do not diverge on mixed-order keys or crafted $R$ points (Reference Paper: Taming the many EdDSAs, Konstantinos Chalkias, François Garillot, and Valeria Nikolaenko, 2020; Reference Paper: ZIP-215: Explicitly Defining and Modifying Ed25519 Validation Rules, Henry de Valence, 2020; Reference Paper: Testing with Wycheproof: Testing cryptographic libraries against known attacks, Daniel Bleichenbacher et al., 2018).
* **Missing Prior Art Comparisons in Resource-Bounded Policy Evaluation and Verifiable Tokens:** In Section 2, the comparison with policy engines is limited to standard Open Policy Agent (OPA) decision logs, omitting closer contemporary baselines that already provide deterministic, resource-bounded offline policy evaluation or Lean 4 mechanization. The authors might consider situating Warrant's `ski@v1` combinator receipts and its open justification-binding gap relative to formally verified evaluators like AWS Cedar, embedded bounded Datalog evaluators in Eclipse Biscuit tokens, and fuel-metered WebAssembly (`policy.wasm`) bundles (Reference Paper: Cedar: A New Language for Expressive, Fast, Safe, and Analyzable Authorization, Joseph W. Cutler et al., 2024; Reference Paper: Eclipse Biscuit Authorization Token Specification, Geoffroy Couprie et al., 2021; Reference Paper: Open Policy Agent (OPA) Bundles, Decision Logs, and Deterministic WebAssembly (Wasm) Compilation, Torin Sandall et al., 2021).
* **Uncontextualized DAG Key Governance and Key-to-Actor Binding Relative to Self-Certifying Prior Art:** The paper derives key rotation, revocation, and threshold governance strictly from causal DAG ancestry rather than wall-clock timestamps (Section 5.3) while noting that base-grade `actor.id` claims can be forged for free without an external trust configuration (Section 8). It would strengthen the paper to compare this governance design with established cryptographic provenance frameworks that also replace wall-clock time with hash-chained causal DAGs while solving key-to-actor binding natively via self-certifying identifiers and pre-rotation commitments (Reference Paper: Key Event Receipt Infrastructure (KERI): Design, Architecture, and Formal Specification, Samuel M. Smith, 2019; Reference Paper: Authentic Chained Data Containers (ACDC), Samuel M. Smith, Philip Feairheller, and Kevin Griffin, 2022; Reference Paper: Keyhive: Local-First Access Control and Causal DAG Key Management, Brooklyn Zelenka, Alex Good, et al., 2024).
* **Reliance on an Unproved Budget-Stability Hypothesis and Vulnerability to Store-Level Blob Withholding:** The Lean 4 settlement mechanization (`proofs/Settlement.lean`) models the evaluator abstractly and relies on an unproved $\Sigma$-GLYPH Book I budget-stability hypothesis as an explicit premise to rule out budget-manipulation attacks yielding distinct eligible results. Furthermore, because `ski@v1` evaluation is a function of $(\mathit{term}, \mathit{atp}, \mathit{store})$ over a plain directory store, an adversary with store write/delete access can selectively withhold a referenced blob to deterministically force an `unresolved` outcome and deactivate a prior settlement without invalidating any `WarrantID` or Ed25519 signature.
* **Underspecified Formal Definitions and Schema Details Across Core Protocol Sections:** Several operational definitions required to implement or verify the protocol from the text alone are described narratively rather than formally—including the exact tuple hashed in baseline versus WRT-005 outcome fingerprints, the recursive Book I value eligibility predicate for nested `DISSONANCE`, the DAG reachability predicate for concurrent key rotations/revocations across unmerged branches, the seven-member verification report schema, and the compositional breakdown of the remaining 48 vectors in the 138-vector conformance pack. Please consider providing concise formal definitions and schema enumerations for these components to make the manuscript fully self-contained.

### [Record Schema, JCS Canonicalization & Domain-Separated Ed25519 Signatures] (Lines: 307-456)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Completeness of the Pinned Ed25519 Verification Specification (Section 4, Lines 440–448):**
   - **Potential Concern:** Section 4 states that Ed25519 signature acceptance is normatively pinned—citing Chalkias et al. (`@chalkias2020`)—by requiring verifiers to reject non-canonical scalars ($S \ge L$), malformed point encodings, and small-order or non-canonically encoded public keys "as a byte-exact blocklist of the 8 canonical torsion encodings plus a $y \ge p$ check." However, to guarantee byte-exact cross-implementation agreement and maintain internal consistency with the empirical findings reported later in Section 6 (Lines 708–709, 751–757), three additional verification criteria from RFC 8032 and Chalkias et al. should be explicitly stated in Section 4:
     1. *Non-canonical negative-zero point encodings ($x = 0$ with sign bit $x_0 = 1$):* The two torsion points with $x = 0$—the identity $(0, 1)$ and the order-2 point $(0, p - 1)$—possess encodings in which the sign bit ($x_0$, the most significant bit of byte 31) is set to $1$ (`0100..0080` and `ecff..ffff`). Because their $y$-coordinates ($1$ and $p - 1$) satisfy $y < p$, neither encoding is caught by a byte-exact blocklist of the *8 canonical* torsion encodings combined solely with a $y \ge p$ check; rejecting them requires enforcing the RFC 8032 §5.1.3 point-decompression rule that $x = 0$ with $x_0 = 1$ is malformed (which is the exact defect reported for the from-scratch Rust verifier in Section 6, Lines 752–754) or checking for small order after decompression.
     2. *Cofactorless vs. cofactored verification equation on mixed-torsion keys:* Blocking the 8 canonical small-order public keys ($[8]A = \mathcal{O}$) eliminates pure-torsion public keys, but does not exclude *mixed-torsion* public keys ($A = A_0 + T$, where $A_0$ has prime order $L$ and $T$ is a non-zero 8-torsion point, yielding order $2L, 4L,$ or $8L$), which Section 6 explicitly tests via 20 mixed-torsion keys in the 472-case differential battery (Lines 708–709, 755–756). Under Chalkias et al.'s classification, implementations that agree on $S < L$, $y < p$, and the 8-point torsion blocklist still diverge on mixed-torsion public keys (or signatures whose commitment point $R$ carries a torsion component) depending on whether they evaluate the cofactorless single-scalar equation ($[S]B = R + [k]A$) or the cofactor-multiplied equation ($[8][S]B = [8]R + [8][k]A$).
     3. *Canonicality and small-order handling of the signature commitment point $R$:* Lines 441–444 specify canonicality and small-order rejection for public keys $A$, but do not state whether the same canonical encoding rules ($y < p$ and no negative zero $x = 0, x_0 = 1$) and/or small-order checks are normatively enforced on the signature commitment point $R$ (the first 32 bytes of the signature).

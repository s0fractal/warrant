# HIGH LEVEL SUMMARY
---
## Summary
This paper presents Warrant (version 1.1.0), a content-addressed, cryptographically signed decision-record format designed for post-hoc evidentiary disputes over autonomous agent actions rather than operational debugging. Each record pins its identity via the SHA-256 of its canonical JSON (JCS/I-JSON) body, signs a 47-byte domain-separated message under strict cofactorless Ed25519 verification rules, forms a directed acyclic graph (DAG) with KERI-inspired key pre-rotation, and supports offline third-party re-execution of stated justifications via a deterministic, budget-bounded combinator calculus (`ski@v1`). The work is accompanied by three byte-exact implementations (Python, Go, and a zero-dependency Rust verifier), a 144-vector conformance suite featuring normative negative batteries, and a Lean 4 mechanization of outcome-fingerprint purity invariants for an experimental dispute-settlement layer.

## Strengths
* The paper addresses a timely and practical accountability gap in deployed autonomous AI systems—moving from mutable, operator-controlled telemetry logs to self-contained, tamper-evident decision receipts whose stated justification computations can be mechanically re-executed offline within bounded resource budgets.
* The specification and conformance methodology demonstrate exceptional engineering rigor, enforcing byte-exact cross-implementation agreement across Python, Go, and zero-dependency Rust verifiers through strict JSON canonicalization, pinned cofactorless Ed25519 equations, differential fuzzing, and normative negative test vector batteries.
* The formalization of an adversarial dispute-settlement and re-litigation calculus over a content-addressed decision DAG, paired with a Lean 4 mechanization of `DISSONANCE`-free outcome-fingerprint invariants, provides an original boundary-defining analysis that clearly separates syntactic outcome novelty from policy-level semantic relevance.
* The manuscript exhibits exemplary scientific candor and epistemic discipline, explicitly stating what a verified record does and does not establish (such as the justification-binding gap, local trust configuration requirements, and external timestamping needs) and verifying every countable claim via a repository checker script.

## Weaknesses
* While the core format proves computational replay (`ski@v1` reduction reproduction), it leaves the semantic "justification-binding gap"—proving that the evaluated computation actually interprets the pinned policy over the cited evidence to entail the decision—as an external authoring convention with an unmerged declaration-coherence profile, offering a narrower guarantee than formal proof-carrying authorization systems (Reference Paper: Proof-Carrying Authentication, Andrew W. Appel and Edward W. Felten, 1999; Reference Paper: Eclipse Biscuit: Authorization Token Specification, Geoffroy Couprie, Clément Delafargue, Eclipse Biscuit Contributors, 2021; Reference Paper: Cedar: A New Language for Expressive, Fast, Safe, and Analyzable Authorization, Joseph W. Cutler et al., 2024).
* Although version 1.1.0 adopts KERI-style key pre-rotation, the unchanged global store-wide conflict rule remains vulnerable to retroactive denial-of-service if any two consecutive *retired* keys ($k_i$ and $k_{i+1}$) in a multi-hop rotation chain ($k_0 \to k_1 \to k_2$) are compromised, and the prose leaves several key-governance and revocation (`supersede`) predicates underspecified for a clean-room implementer (Reference Paper: Key Event Receipt Infrastructure (KERI), Samuel M. Smith, 2019).
* In the settlement layer, the Lean 4 mechanization of the unadopted WRT-005 outcome-fingerprint rule relies on an unproved Book I budget-stability hypothesis and lacks a formal refinement proof to the Python, Go, and Rust evaluators, while the operational role of per-blob "foreclosure" relative to whole-tunnel blob set membership remains somewhat ambiguous.
* Several specification and empirical details needed for independent clean-room reimplementation and practical evaluation—such as the exact boundary between fatal recursive unknown-field rejection and non-fatal co-signature filtering, the evaluation cadence of local resource fences, the categorization of the 11 reference defects found during the Rust port, fault-injection verification for 9 of the 11 contract-checker refusals, and wall-clock/memory benchmarks—would benefit from fuller exposition.

### [Record Schema, Canonicalization Rules, and Ed25519 Signature Semantics] (Pages: 6-8)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Potential Concern: Ambiguity in how the mixed-torsion edge signature vectors are constructed (Section 4, page 8).**
   In Section 4 ("Acceptance is pinned, equation included"), the text describes the five edge signature vectors added in version 1.1.0 to separate cofactorless verifiers from cofactored or scalar-reducing verifiers:
   > "...and a mixed-torsion key $A + T_8$ with one signature made for it where $[k]T_8 \neq \mathcal{O}$ (must not verify) and one where $[k]T_8 = \mathcal{O}$ (must verify). Each of the four negative ones verifies under a cofactored or reducing verifier and fails under the specified one."

   This mathematical separation relies on an unstated assumption about how a signature $(R, S)$ is "made for" the mixed-torsion public key $A' = A + T_8$ (where $A = [a]B$ lies in the prime-order subgroup and $T_8$ is an 8-torsion point):
   - Because $T_8$ has no discrete logarithm with respect to the base point $B$, a standard Ed25519 scalar signer computes $R = [r]B$ and $S = (r + ka) \bmod L$ using the prime-order secret scalar $a$ of $A$, while binding the modified public key $A' = A + T_8$ inside the challenge $k = \text{SHA-512}(R \parallel A' \parallel M) \bmod L$. Under this construction, the left-hand side of the cofactorless verification equation $[S]B = R + [k]A'$ evaluates to $[r]B + [ka]B = R + [k]A$, whereas the right-hand side evaluates to $R + [k]A + [k]T_8$. Consequently, the cofactorless equation holds if and only if $[k]T_8 = \mathcal{O}$ (i.e., $k \equiv 0 \pmod{\text{ord}(T_8)}$, achieved by selecting the nonce $r$ or message $M$ accordingly), while the cofactored equation $[8][S]B = [8]R + [8][k]A'$ always holds because $[8]T_8 = \mathcal{O}$.
   - However, without stating how $(R, S)$ is generated, "a signature made for $A + T_8$" could alternatively be interpreted as one where the ephemeral commitment $R$ also incorporates a compensating torsion shift (e.g., $R' = [r]B - [k]T_8$), which would satisfy the cofactorless equation even when $[k]T_8 \neq \mathcal{O}$.

   Explicitly stating that these two vectors use $R = [r]B$ and $S = (r + ka) \bmod L$ computed from the prime-order scalar $a$ with $A + T_8$ hashed into $k$—analogous to how $S = ka$ is explicitly specified for the small-order $R$ vectors immediately preceding them—would make the mathematical derivation self-contained and unambiguous.

2. **Potential Concern: Boundary between fatal recursive unknown-field rejection and non-fatal signature-entry filtering for clean-room reimplementation (Sections 3 and 4, pages 6–8).**
   The paper highlights a clean-room implementation by an independent party "who has only the text" (`NEED-002`) as an open validation goal. For an implementer working from the paper text or evaluating the specification mechanics across Sections 3 and 4, two structural details interact in a way that is not disambiguated in the prose:
   - **Recursive Unknown-Field Rule vs. Hostile Co-Signature Appends:** Section 3 (page 6) states that "unknown fields make the record invalid, recursively, at every level of the tree." Section 4 (page 7) defines a stored warrant as an envelope containing "the body plus a signature array, each entry an object of exactly `actor`, `key` (the 64-hex public key) and `sig` (the 128-hex signature)," and later (page 8) states that because anyone with store write access can append junk, "An entry that is not even an object is treated the same way: it is reported as a warning and excluded, and only the absence of any valid signature by the filing actor makes the record an error." It is not clear from the text whether an unknown field inside an individual signature entry object (e.g., `{actor, key, sig, extra}`)—or an unknown top-level field on the outer envelope object—triggers a fatal record validation error (under Section 3's recursive rule) or is dropped as a non-fatal warning (under Section 4's hostile-append rule).
   - **Nested Sub-Schemas and Pinned Diagnostic Constants:** Section 3 lists the nine top-level fields of `body` (`warrant`, `decision`, `subject`, `under`, `because`, `evidence`, `actor`, `prior`, `ts`), while later paragraphs reference subfields such as `subject.hash`, `subject.note`, and `actor.id` (pages 6–8), `check` and `transcript` references inside `because` (page 6), and a "pinned diagnostic string" required when rejecting a legacy bare-digest signature (page 8). Clarifying the exact boundary between fatal schema validation and non-fatal signature-array filtering in Section 4—and explicitly noting in Section 3 that the complete recursive field grammar and diagnostic constants reside in the companion specification document in the repository rather than the paper prose alone—would prevent potential divergence in clean-room implementations.

*(Verification Summary: Aside from the points of clarification above, the technical claims in Sections 3 and 4 were verified as sound, including the $\text{WarrantID} = \text{SHA-256}(\text{JCS}(\text{body}))$ construction, UTF-16 code-unit sorting vs. ASCII byte order, the $\pm(2^{53}-1)$ integer domain restriction aligning RFC 8785 and RFC 7493, the four JSON leniency-split rules, the 47-byte `"warrant-sig-v1:" || WarrantID_raw` domain separation and flag-day migration rationale, the 8-entry canonical small-order public key blocklist, and the canonical point-encoding checks $y < p$ and rejection of $x = 0$ with the sign bit set covering `0100..0080` and `ecff..ffff`.)*

### [Re-Executable Reasons, Settlement Calculus, and DAG Key Governance] (Pages: 9-13)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Potential Concern: Completeness and Security Subtlety of Key-Governance Predicates under Pre-Rotation (Section 5.3, pp. 11–12)**
   - **Location:** Page 11, Section 5.3, paragraphs 1–3.
   - **Observation:** Section 5.3 defines two key-governance operations: a rotation (`accept` warrant whose subject is the new key blob, carrying proof-of-possession by the incoming key and authorization under the current key policy) and a revocation (`supersede` of the rotation that introduced the key). To close the v1.0.0 vulnerability—where a leaked retired key `a_old` could sign a competing rotation `a_old -> a_fork` at an ancestor DAG position where `a_old` was current, triggering a store-wide conflict ("authorized rotations for one actor that are maximal and mutually unordered") that retroactively invalidates the actor's entire signature history back to genesis—v1.1.0 introduces KERI-style pre-rotation:
     > "Every key state commits in advance to the SHA-256 of its successor: genesis keys through the trust configuration, and every later key through the key blob that introduced it. A rotation is authorized only if its incoming key matches the commitment in force at its position, and a key state without a commitment is terminal. This is added to the existing rules and replaces none of them. A retired key alone can then authorize nothing at any position, which a three-implementation countervector now checks. The residual is stated rather than hidden. A holder of a key and its committed successor can still introduce that successor a second time at the same position with a different onward commitment, and that is a conflict. Since the successor is the current or a later signing key, this takes a compromise that already lets the attacker sign as the actor."
   - **Substantive Impact:** A reader or clean-room implementer (`NEED-002`) evaluating this security argument or reimplementing the key-governance predicates from the text alone encounters four related logical and specification gaps:
     1. *Cryptographic binding of the pre-rotation digest, proof-of-possession, and duplicate rotation records:* Once `a_old` legitimately rotates to `a_new` via rotation warrant `R_1`, the key blob for `a_new` is public in the store, and at the ancestor DAG position where `a_old` was current, the commitment in force is still the digest of `a_new`. First, for an attacker holding both keys to introduce `a_new` a second time "with a different onward commitment," the pre-rotation `SHA-256` digest must cover only the incoming public key (or a subset of the key blob excluding its onward commitment) rather than the entire canonical incoming key blob, which the text does not specify. Second, if an attacker holds *only* the leaked `a_old`, what prevents them from constructing a second rotation warrant `R_2` at the old DAG position pointing to the *exact same* already-public `a_new` key blob (varying `ts`, `subject.note`, or `evidence` to produce a distinct `WarrantID`)? Unless `a_new`'s proof-of-possession signature covers the enclosing rotation warrant's `WarrantID` (so that creating `R_2` requires `a_new`'s private key) or the conflict predicate deduplicates rotations by target key state rather than by distinct maximal rotation `WarrantID`s, `a_old` alone could still create two maximal mutually unordered rotation records (`R_1` and `R_2`, or `R_2` and a later rotation `R_3: a_new -> a_next`) and trigger a store-wide conflict.
     2. *Multi-hop rotation chains (`a_0 -> a_1 -> a_2`) and store-wide conflict scope:* The text states that the residual two-key attack *"takes a compromise that already lets the attacker sign as the actor"* because *"the successor is the current or a later signing key."* However, once an actor has rotated at least twice (`r_1: a_0 -> a_1` followed by `r_2: a_1 -> a_2`), both `a_0` and its committed successor `a_1` are *retired* keys, whereas `a_2` is the current active key. Compromising consecutive retired keys `a_0` and `a_1` does *not* allow the attacker to sign as the active key `a_2`, yet it still allows the attacker to introduce `a_1` a second time at `a_0`'s DAG position (`r_1': a_0 -> a_1'`) with a different onward commitment. Because `r_1'` has no descendant rotations, `r_1'` and `r_2` are maximal and mutually unordered. And because pre-rotation "is added to the existing rules and replaces none of them," the unchanged store-wide conflict rule still retroactively marks genesis and all records signed by the uncompromised current key `a_2` as unbound, rather than scoping the conflict to the causal future (or non-ancestors) of the diverging fork.
     3. *Unspecified predicates for revocation (`supersede`):* While paragraph 1 introduces revocation as a `supersede` of the rotation that introduced a key, paragraphs 2–3 specify authorization and pre-rotation predicates solely for rotations (`accept`). The text does not state which key at which DAG position is required to authorize a `supersede` of `a_old -> a_new`, what prevents a leaked retired key `a_old` from unilaterally signing a `supersede` at the parent DAG position where `a_old` was active, or what key state and onward commitment remain in force after a rotation is superseded (since `a_old`'s single pre-rotation commitment was already bound to `a_new`).
     4. *Liveness under pre-committed key loss or compromise:* Under a strict single-hash pre-rotation commitment, if the pre-committed successor key is accidentally lost or compromised *prior* to rotation, an actor without a multi-party threshold recovery mechanism has no way to rotate to an alternative successor, rendering the current key state involuntarily terminal.
   - **Suggestion:** Explicitly specify in Section 5.3: (a) what exact bytes the pre-rotation `SHA-256` commitment and the incoming key's proof-of-possession signature cover, and whether conflict is evaluated over distinct rotation `WarrantID`s or distinct target key blobs; (b) that in a multi-hop chain (`a_0 -> a_1 -> a_2`), the residual store-wide conflict requires compromising two consecutive *retired* keys (`a_0` and `a_1`) rather than necessarily the *current* key (`a_2`), along with the design rationale for retaining store-wide retroactive invalidation over causal-cone conflict scoping; (c) the exact authorization and commitment-transition predicates for `supersede` warrants; and (d) the operational assumption regarding the loss of a pre-committed successor key prior to rotation.

2. **Potential Concern: Operational Role of "Blob Foreclosure" vs. Re-Litigation Branches (a) and (b), and Scope of the Mechanized Re-Opening Theorem (Section 5.2, pp. 10–11)**
   - **Location:** Pages 10–11, Section 5.2, paragraphs 2, 3, and 6.
   - **Observation:** In Section 5.2 (page 10), paragraph 2 states:
     > "A blob **forecloses** only the claims some check in the tunnel actually evaluated over it — mere presence in an evidence list forecloses nothing, and an unresolvable blob forecloses nothing, because what cannot be read cannot have been reasoned over. A re-litigation record must carry either (a) an evidence hash absent from the tunnel, or (b) a check, all of whose blobs resolve, that re-runs to an outcome fingerprint not present in the tunnel."
     Immediately afterward, paragraph 3 clarifies: *"First, branch (a) tests absence from the tunnel's whole blob set: every blob a tunnel record cites, whether or not anything foreclosed it... Second, the specification defines foreclosure by 'the claims some reason in the tunnel actually makes about' a blob, but defines no read set for either runtime."* Later, on page 11, paragraph 1 summarizes the Lean 4 mechanization (`proofs/Settlement.lean`) of proposal WRT-005 as proving two invariants: *"a fingerprint is a function of the eligible result value and nothing a filer writes, and a settled matter cannot be re-opened without a new demonstrated value."*
   - **Substantive Impact:** Two points of ambiguity arise from these passages:
     1. *Algorithmic effect of blob foreclosure:* Because branch (a) tests absence from the tunnel's *entire* cited blob set ("whether or not anything foreclosed it"), mere presence of a blob hash in a tunnel record's `evidence` list *does* prevent a later record from re-opening the settlement via that blob under branch (a), even if no check in the tunnel ever evaluated it or if the blob was unresolvable. Similarly, branch (b) checks only whether all blobs of the new check resolve and its outcome fingerprint is absent from the tunnel. Aside from unresolvable settlement-critical references rendering a settling record settlement-inactive (noted in Section 8, page 17), it is unclear whether the per-blob "foreclosure" rule has any mechanical effect in the Python, Go, and Rust verifiers, or whether it is currently an unenforced conceptual clause in the specification that the implementations approximate via whole-tunnel blob set membership due to the absence of a runtime read set.
     2. *Scope of the mechanized re-opening theorem:* Because WRT-005 hardens branch (b) only—while branch (a) evidence-based re-opening remains satisfiable at zero computational cost by any fresh evidence blob unless restricted by the active settlement policy—stating on page 11 that *"a settled matter cannot be re-opened without a new demonstrated value"* reads slightly more broadly than what the mechanism enforces unless qualified as applying specifically to branch (b) check-based re-litigation.
   - **Suggestion:** Clarify in Section 5.2 whether the per-blob "foreclosure" definition currently gates any active check in the verification algorithm (or is an aspirational specification clause approximated by whole-tunnel cited blob set membership), and specify on page 11 that the mechanized re-opening invariant applies to branch (b) check-fingerprint re-litigation while branch (a) remains governed by the active settlement policy.

3. **Potential Concern: Underspecified Evaluation Cadence and Counting Semantics for Local Host Resource Fences (Section 5.1, p. 9)**
   - **Location:** Page 9, Section 5.1, paragraph 4.
   - **Observation:** Section 5.1 notes that in addition to the default $10^8$ ATP budget, a verifier fences materialized state during `ski@v1` re-execution:
     > "The reference implementations both stop at a node depth of 4096, $10^6$ materialized nodes and $10^6$ store fetches, checked at the same moments, and the Rust port reproduces the Python limits and their cadence because a limit checked at a different moment produces a different report... Verifiers that interoperate should use the reference fences so that they agree by default."
   - **Substantive Impact:** Under Section 5.4 (page 12), a check that trips a resource fence (`ski@v1 unverified`) is escalated from `WARN` to `ERR` when it belongs to a settlement-active record under settlement-grade verification, changing the top-level `ok` boolean (`errors == 0`). While the numerical thresholds ($4096$, $10^6$, $10^6$) are explicitly stated, the *cadence* and counting semantics—the exact points in the $\Sigma$-GLYPH Book I reduction loop or AST traversal where node depth, materialized node count (e.g., cumulative allocated nodes vs. peak live nodes), and store fetches are measured and checked, and the precedence when a fence and budget exhaustion or missing blob occur on the same step—are not described in the paper. Leaving the cadence implicit in the Python and Rust codebases creates a reproducibility gap at the fence boundaries for an independent clean-room implementation from the text alone (`NEED-002`).
   - **Suggestion:** Briefly state the exact points in the reduction cycle at which the three fences are measured and checked (or explicitly clarify whether the step-by-step cadence and counting semantics are normatively pinned in the repository's specification document rather than only in the reference implementations).

4. **Potential Concern: Deterministic Ordering vs. Multiset Agreement and `subject` Formatting in the Verification Report (Section 5.4, p. 12)**
   - **Location:** Page 12, Section 5.4, paragraph 1.
   - **Observation:** Section 5.4 specifies that the machine-readable report has seven members (`report`, `grade`, `ok`, `records`, `errors`, `warnings`, `findings`, where each finding has a `level` of `ERR` or `WARN`, a `subject`, and a non-normative `message`), requires that *"Counts must equal findings, order must be deterministic,"* and states that *"Two implementations must agree on the counts and the multiset of (severity, subject) pairs."* In Section 6 (page 13), the paper further notes that the Rust verifier's settlement-grade reports are *"byte-identical to the reference's."*
   - **Substantive Impact:** For a clean-room implementer, two schema details are left slightly ambiguous: (i) whether the specification pins a canonical sort key for the `findings` array (so any two conforming implementations produce identical array ordering) or only requires each implementation to be internally deterministic while cross-implementation conformance is evaluated over the order-independent multiset of `(level, subject)` pairs; and (ii) how the `subject` string is formatted for findings that do not correspond to a valid `WarrantID` (such as store-level failures, malformed trust configurations, actor-level key conflicts, or record files that fail JSON parsing before a `WarrantID` can be computed).
   - **Suggestion:** Briefly clarify the canonical sort key for `findings` (if pinned by the specification) and how `subject` is populated for store-level, actor-level, and unparseable-file findings.

#### Minor Corrections and Typos

- **Page 10, Section 5.2, Paragraphs 2–3:** Paragraph 2 paraphrases the foreclosure rule as *"A blob forecloses only the claims some **check** in the tunnel actually evaluated over it,"* whereas Paragraph 3 quotes the specification as defining foreclosure by *"the claims some **reason** in the tunnel actually makes about"* a blob. ### [Multi-Implementation Conformance Testing, Fuzzing, and Review Ledger] (Pages: 13-16)
#### Potential Mistakes and Improvements

1. **Incomplete Negative-Control Verification of the Specification-to-Test Contract Checker (Section 6, Pages 14–15):**
   - **Potential Concern:** At the end of Section 6, the text establishes the methodological principle that *"a green that cannot be shown capable of turning red, for the stated reason, is not evidence,"* motivated by two prior negative controls in the repository that failed when tested (one that went green having tested nothing, and one that *"turned red at the wrong step, which proves nothing about the step it claims to cover"*). Immediately following this, the paper introduces the version 1.1.0 specification-to-test contract checker—which enforces eleven distinct refusal conditions—and discloses: *"Each of the checker's eleven refusals has a control, a broken copy that must be refused. Two of the refusals were then disabled to show that their controls turn red. The rest have not had that done yet."* While the candor of this disclosure aligns with the paper's reporting discipline, leaving 9 of the 11 refusal controls unverified against disabled checks is a potential methodological gap under the section's own evidentiary criterion: without fault-injection verification, the presence of a broken-copy fixture only verifies that the control fails *some* refusal path, not that it fails at the specific refusal condition it is meant to witness.
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Because the eleven broken-copy fixtures already exist and the checker is a local, deterministic script, automating a self-test or mutation loop that disables each of the remaining 9 refusal rules one at a time and asserts that its corresponding control transitions from refused to accepted (or asserting that each broken copy triggers a distinct, rule-specific diagnostic code) is a bounded step that would bring all 11 controls up to the stated standard.

2. **Reconciling the Prose Negative-Battery Counts with the Conformance Pack Table (`validate`, `parse`, and `verify-sig` in Section 6, Page 13):**
   - **Verification Summary:** The internal arithmetic of the 144-vector conformance pack v1.3.0 table on page 13 is exact: the base-grade rows sum to $52 + 21 + 20 + 33 + 4 + 4 + 6 = 140$, the settlement-grade rows sum to $3 + 1 = 4$, and the `must reject` column sums to $0 + 16 + 15 + 28 + 0 + 0 + 5 + 2 + 0 = 66$. Likewise, the `canon` row ($52 = 48\text{-case battery} + 4\text{ pinned record vectors}$) and the `verify-sig` row ($33\text{ total} = 5\text{ valid} + 14\text{ weak-key rejects} + 10\text{ message-construction rejects} + 4\text{ v1.1.0 cofactor/scalar edge rejects} = 5\text{ valid} + 28\text{ must reject}$) are fully consistent with Section 4.
   - **Potential Concern:** Under *Vectors, positive and negative* (page 13, as well as in the Abstract on page 2), the prose states that the normative negative batteries include *"15 bodies for which validation must return an error."* However, in the conformance pack table directly below, the `validate` class lists `21` total vectors with `16` in the `must reject` column, while the `parse` class lists `20` total vectors with `15` in the `must reject` column. Unlike `canon` (where the prose explicitly bridges the 48-case battery to the 52-vector row), the text does not explain how the "15 bodies" in the prose map onto the `16` `must reject` vectors in `validate` versus the `15` `must reject` vectors in `parse` (e.g., whether `validate` includes the 15 body-validation errors from the specification's negative battery plus 1 envelope-level or pack-level validation error, and what the 15 `parse` rejections cover).
   - **Suggested Improvement:** Add a brief parenthetical or clause under *The pack* clarifying how the 15 invalid bodies in the specification's negative battery map to the 16 `must reject` entries in `validate` and the 15 `must reject` entries in `parse` (and explicitly noting that the 14 weak-key and 14 signature-construction negative batteries sum to the 28 `must reject` entries in `verify-sig`).

3. **Unspecified Categories for the Eleven Reference Defects Found During the Rust Port (Section 6, Page 13):**
   - **Potential Concern:** Under *Three implementations* (page 13), the text states: *"Porting it [the Rust verifier] to that standard found eleven defects in the reference (Section 10)."* However, Section 10 (page 18) repeats nearly the exact same sentence (*"The port found eleven defects in the reference, which were fixed in all implementations"*) without enumerating or categorizing them, and the bulleted list under *What the harnesses caught* (page 14) focuses primarily on earlier Python–Go fuzzing defects, Go consensus splits, and defects in the Rust Ed25519 implementation itself. Because discovering 11 defects in a reference implementation during a clean-room, zero-dependency port is a primary empirical validation of the paper's multi-implementation design rule, leaving those 11 defects uncharacterized is a potential clarity gap in the empirical narrative.
